The best cold call discovery questions are short, open and about the present: how the prospect handles something today, what they would change, and how long it has been that way. Ask two or three of them in the first two minutes, make the second one out of the prospect's own answer, and stop as soon as you have a reason to ask for fifteen minutes. A cold call is not a discovery meeting. Its job is to find one real problem fast enough that the prospect is still on the line when you ask for a time. Below are ten questions, each matched to the moment it works, with what a good answer gives you and what to do with it.

## Why a list of questions fails

The usual cold call script has a block in the middle labelled "discovery" with five to eight questions in it. It fails for three reasons.

The prospect did not ask for the call. They are mildly irritated, and every question after the second feels like a toll. By the fifth they are looking for the exit.

The questions are in the wrong order. They were written at a desk, not in the order a conversation goes, so "What is your budget?" lands before the prospect has said a word about a problem.

The rep never uses the answers. A question the rep asks and does not build on is a question wasted. If the prospect says the onboarding takes three months and the rep's next line is question four from the list, the best moment of the call has gone past.

So think of discovery on a cold call as a chain, not a list. One question opens, the answer gives you a word to pick up, and the follow-up uses that word. Two links are usually enough.

## Ten questions, by moment

The questions are written for a call that has already stated its reason, which is where discovery starts. If you have not stated a reason yet, our post on [the reason for the call](/blog/reason-for-call) comes first.

| # | The moment | The question | What a good answer unlocks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Reason stated, prospect neutral | "How does your team handle [the process] at the moment?" | Their current workflow, the tool they use, who owns it |
| 2 | They say it is fine | "What would you change about it if you could?" | The gap, in their words |
| 3 | They name a problem | "How long has that been the case?" | Whether it is new energy or an old habit |
| 4 | Problem named, no sense of weight | "What does that mean for the team in a normal week?" | The cost, without asking for a number |
| 5 | They mention a tool | "What do you mostly use it for, and what does it not do?" | The reason a conversation could be worth having |
| 6 | Everything is vague | "When did it last cause you a problem?" | A real, recent example |
| 7 | They mention a trigger | "What changed this year that made it matter?" | The reason for acting now, if there is one |
| 8 | You need to find who else is involved | "Who else feels it when that goes wrong?" | The other people and roles, without asking "are you the decision maker?" |
| 9 | You are not sure it is a priority | "Is that on your list this quarter, or further down?" | Whether to book now or log a callback |
| 10 | They have said enough, time to ask | "If we spent fifteen minutes on it, what would you want it to cover?" | The agenda, and a bridge to the close |

You will use two or three of these on any call, never ten. The table is a menu. Question 10 is a different kind from the other nine because it is the question that leads into the ask, and it works best when the prospect has already answered one or two of the others.

## What each one sounds like

Questions 1 to 3 open the topic. Question 1 sounds plain on purpose: it is easy to answer and does not give away a view. Question 2 is for the prospect who says "it's fine", because contentment usually has an exception. Question 3 tells you how live the problem is. "Since we hired in the spring" is a live problem. "Oh, years" is a tolerated one, and you should adjust the ask accordingly.

Questions 4 and 5 go a level deeper. Question 4 gets the weight of the problem without asking for numbers, which prospects rarely give to a stranger. Question 5 is for "we already use X": the answer hands you the gap, so never argue with the tool they already have. Our guide to [the eight cold call objections you hear most](/blog/common-cold-call-objections) has the full shape of that exchange.

Questions 6 to 8 deal with vague answers, triggers and other people. Question 6 turns a general feeling into a specific event, and specific events are what meetings get booked on. Question 8 replaces "are you the decision maker?" with a question about who else is affected. The answer is usually a name and a role.

Questions 9 and 10 point towards the close. If the answer to 9 is "further down", book a callback rather than a meeting. Question 10 asks the prospect to write the agenda, and whatever they say becomes the first line of your invite.

## Two links of the chain, in a real exchange

Here is how a chain works on a call. The rep has stated a reason and asked question 1.

> Rep: How does your team handle new-hire onboarding at the moment?
>
> Prospect: It's mostly shadowing. They sit with someone for a few weeks.
>
> Rep: A few weeks of shadowing. What does that mean for the person they sit with?
>
> Prospect: Honestly, they lose about half their week.
>
> Rep: How long has it been like that?
>
> Prospect: Since we doubled the team in the spring.
>
> Rep: That's the thing I called about. If we spent fifteen minutes on it, what would you want it to cover?

All four questions are in the table, but the rep chose each one from what the prospect had just said. The second, third and fourth were built from the prospect's own words: "shadowing", "half their week", "since the spring". That is what the chain means. The rep never changed subject, never asked about budget and never started the pitch.

And here is the harder version, with a prospect who gives three-word answers.

> Rep: How does your team handle new-hire onboarding at the moment?
>
> Prospect: It's fine.
>
> Rep: Good to hear. What would you change about it if you could?
>
> Prospect: Nothing really.
>
> Rep: Fair enough. When did it last cause you a problem?
>
> Prospect: Can't remember.
>
> Rep: Then it sounds like it isn't a priority, which is fine. Is it worth me checking back next quarter?

Three questions, no engagement, and the rep stopped. That is the right call. A prospect who answers three times in three words has told you the problem is not live. Pushing a fourth and fifth question burns goodwill you can use next quarter.

## Questions to leave out

A few questions lose more calls than they win. None of them are banned forever. They just do not belong in the first two minutes.

**"What is your budget?"** There is no project yet, so there is no budget. The question puts the prospect on the defensive and tells you nothing. Budget is a conversation for the meeting.

**"Are you the decision maker?"** It sounds like a credentials check. Ask who else is affected, as in question 8, and the real answer arrives without the insult.

**"What is your biggest challenge?"** Too wide. The prospect has forty and will pick the safest. A question about a specific process gets a specific answer.

**Questions that answer themselves.** "I imagine onboarding takes a lot of time, right?" is an opinion in the shape of a question. The prospect says yes to be polite and you leave with false information.

**More than one question at a time.** Two questions in a row get one answer, and it is the easier one. Ask, then stop.

## How to listen so the follow-up is there

The follow-up is the skill, and it depends on listening for one word. Pick up the most specific noun or phrase in the prospect's answer and put it in your next question. "Shadowing", "half their week", "since the spring". Someone who hears their own words said back feels heard, which is the reverse of feeling interrogated.

After the question, stop talking. Two or three seconds of silence feels long on the phone, and it is when the prospect decides whether to say more. If you fill it you answer on their behalf.

If you are talking more than the prospect in the discovery stretch, you are making statements disguised as questions. Our post on [how much an SDR should talk on a cold call](/blog/cold-call-talk-ratio) covers why the longest stretch matters more than the overall ratio.

## How to tell if your discovery is working

A manager scoring discovery from a transcript can use a simple test. For each question the rep asked, does the next line use something the prospect said? If it does not, the question was from a list.

On a scale of 0 to 10, a rep who asks no questions or strings together yes-or-no questions sits near a 2. A rep who asks open questions from a list with no follow-up sits near a 5. A rep who asks problem-focused questions tied to the prospect's world and builds on what they said sits near a 7. A 9 is two or three sharp questions that surface a real problem with no checklist in sight. Those anchors come from the [cold call scorecard](/blog/cold-call-scorecard-template), where discovery carries 15% of the overall score. It carries less than the close and the reason for the call because on a cold call discovery is the bridge between them, not the destination.

A rep who is weak here usually needs one thing: the second question. Managers who want to see which reps stop at question one can see discovery next to the other five skills for every rep on the [page for enablement teams](/for-enablement).
