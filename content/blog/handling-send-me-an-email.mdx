When a prospect says "just send me an email", agree in two words, ask what the email would have to answer to be worth reading, answer that in one sentence, and ask for fifteen minutes at a specific time. The line is a polite way of ending the call, not a request for information, and the prospect expects you to accept it. Folding gets you an email nobody opens. Arguing gets you hung up on. Asking what to put in the email makes the prospect say their real question out loud, with you still on the line, which is the conversation you called for in the first place.

Most reps fold. They say "sure, what's the best address?", send an email that is never opened, and log the call as a follow-up. Nothing was lost except the meeting. The response that works treats the line as what it is, a soft no, and answers the no rather than the request.

## Why folding feels like progress

The prospect has offered you a graceful exit. You got an email address, you have a reason to follow up, and the call ended without anyone being rude. Every one of those is a consolation prize.

Gong's cold-call data is clear on what a booked meeting looks like. [Successful cold calls averaged 5 minutes 50 seconds and unsuccessful ones 3 minutes 14 seconds](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats), and the difference was back-and-forth. The [median successful cold call lasts 4.8 minutes and handles at least two objections](https://www.gong.io/blog/we-found-the-top-objections-across-300m-cold-calls-heres-how-to-handle-them-all). "Send me an email" is often the first of those two, and a rep who folds never finds out what the second would have been.

## What the line usually means

[Cognism ranks "send me an email" the fourth most common objection on cold calls](https://www.cognism.com/state-of-cold-calling), behind "I'm busy", "not interested" and "I'm the wrong person". Gong found that the [five most common objections account for 74% of all objections, and about half of them are brush-offs](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-hidden-power-of-cold-calling-insights-from-300m-calls) rather than real concerns. "Send me an email" sits squarely in the brush-off half.

A prospect who says "not interested" has taken a position. A prospect who says "send me an email" has asked for something, and you can ask what. Gong's top reps [answer an objection with a clarifying question 54.3% of the time, against 31% for average reps](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-objections). On this objection the question writes itself.

The mistake to name is answering the request instead of the no. The rep hears "email" and starts solving for the email. The prospect said "email" but meant "goodbye". Solve for the goodbye.

## The three responses side by side

| Response | Fold | Fight | Redirect |
|---|---|---|---|
| What the rep says | "Sure, what's the best address?" | "I'd rather not send an email. Can I just ask you a couple of quick questions?" | "Happy to. So it's not something you delete, what's the one thing you'd want it to answer?" |
| What the prospect hears | Relief. The call is over | A telemarketer who will not take a hint | A reasonable person asking a reasonable question |
| What happens next | An unopened email and a follow-up task | A harder no, often a hang-up | The prospect names a question. The rep answers it and asks for a time |
| What the transcript shows | Objection accepted at the first ask | Objection argued with | Objection acknowledged, isolated, redirected |

The last row matters for managers. On a scorecard that grades acknowledge, isolate and redirect, the redirect column scores well even when the prospect still says no.

## The four steps

**1. Agree, briefly.**
Fighting the request makes you sound like a telemarketer. "Happy to." One beat, no more. Gong's top performers [pause about 1.6 seconds before answering an objection, five times longer than average reps](https://www.gong.io/blog/objection-handling-techniques). That figure is from sales meetings rather than cold calls, but the behaviour transfers. Rushing in sounds like a rebuttal even when the words are not.

**2. Ask what to put in it.**
This is the whole technique. "So I don't send you something useless, what's the one thing you'd actually want to see in it?" Now the prospect has to think about their problem, out loud, with you on the line. The ones who say "anything is fine" have told you they are not a prospect.

**3. Answer the thing they said, in one sentence.**
They said "pricing"? "It's per vehicle per month, typically forty to eighty, and I can tell you where you'd land in about two questions." They said "how it works"? Give the one-line mechanism, then stop. A paragraph here turns the opening back into a pitch.

**4. Ask for the small next step, with a time.**
"Rather than go back and forth over email, I've got fifteen minutes Thursday at ten. If it isn't relevant you'll know in five." In our calls a specific slot converts far better than "sometime next week", because "sometime" is another way of saying "send me an email".

## The words, together

> Prospect: Just send me an email.
>
> Rep: Happy to. So it's not something you delete, what's the one thing you'd want it to answer?
>
> Prospect: Honestly, whether it works with what we already have.
>
> Rep: Fair. It sits next to your existing tooling rather than replacing it, and most teams keep both. Quickest way to see if that's true for you is fifteen minutes on Thursday at ten. Does that work, or is Friday better?
>
> Prospect: Thursday's no good. Friday morning, maybe.
>
> Rep: Friday at half nine. I'll send the invite now, and the email you asked for underneath it.

And the branch where the prospect is not a prospect.

> Prospect: Just send me an email.
>
> Rep: Happy to. What's the one thing you'd want it to answer?
>
> Prospect: Whatever you've got is fine.
>
> Rep: That usually means it isn't the right time, which is fair. Who on your side would own driver compliance if it came up?
>
> Prospect: That'd be Priya in ops.
>
> Rep: Thanks, I'll try Priya. I won't send the email.

The second exchange did not book a meeting. It got a name and a reason to call that name, and it took a few seconds longer than folding would have.

## When to actually send the email

If they answer step two with something real and still decline the meeting, send the email. Send exactly what they asked for, in three sentences, with the meeting link at the bottom. Then call again in a week. You are no longer a cold call. You are the person who did what they said.

The call also makes the email get read. Gong found that [cold calls in a sequence nearly double email reply rates, from 1.81% to 3.44%, even when the call did not connect](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-hidden-power-of-cold-calling-insights-from-300m-calls). Chorus found a [successful cold-calling sequence averaged about four calls over 20 days](http://blog.chorus.ai/do-you-have-at-least-4-calls-in-your-cold-calling-sequence). An email that answers a named question, between two calls, gets a reply. An email that answers no question, after a fold, gets deleted.

The same shape works for the rest of the brush-off family, and our guide to [the eight cold call objections you will hear most](/blog/common-cold-call-objections) gives the words for each. For a manager the question is which of the three columns the team defaults to.
