A 30-60-90 day SDR onboarding plan splits the first quarter into three phases, each with a different job. Days 1 to 30 are for learning the conversation: who the buyer is, the first thirty seconds, the objections the team hears most, and a hundred scored practice calls before any real account hears the rep. Days 31 to 60 are for real dials on a low-stakes list with a tight feedback loop, forty dials a day and three reviewed calls a day. Days 61 to 90 are for production on the real list, with a meetings target that rises each week and coaching the rep runs themselves. The plan is built around one idea: the rep should have made a hundred bad cold calls before anyone in your pipeline hears one.

## The three phases on one page

| Phase | Goal | The number that says it is done |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1 to 30 | Learn the conversation away from the pipeline | Over 100 practice calls, six dimension scores trending up, a next step from a resistant prospect about one call in five |
| Days 31 to 60 | Real dials on a low-stakes list, every call reviewed | Conversations over two minutes rising as a share of connects, week on week |
| Days 61 to 90 | Production with a target, rep-led coaching | Meetings held against a weekly target that rises towards a tenured rep's |

The hundred calls and the one-in-five are our observation, not industry figures. The industry figure is the overall ramp. [The Bridge Group's 2025 SDR Metrics report](https://www.bridgegroupinc.com/research/2025-sdr-models-metrics-report-the-bridge-group) puts average ramp at 3.0 months, with 60% of SDRs hitting quota at the median company. The plan below is how to spend those three months.

## The mistake to design around

The expensive way to onboard an SDR is to let them learn on the live pipeline. Every account a new rep burns in month one is an account someone else could have booked in month four, and the rep learns almost nothing from those calls because nobody hears them. They get a hang-up, they do not know why, they move to the next name.

Ramping on the pipeline also teaches that the point is volume, and a rep who learns to dial fast before they can hold a conversation keeps dialling fast. So the plan separates practice from production. Our post on [average SDR ramp time](/blog/average-sdr-ramp-time) goes through where the weeks usually go.

## Days 1 to 30: learn the conversation

**Week 1. Who we sell to and why they buy.**
Not the product. The buyer. The rep should be able to describe three target personas, what each one is measured on, and what goes wrong in their week that your product fixes. Have them write a one-paragraph reason for calling for each persona and say it out loud to you. If it contains the word "solution", send it back.

**Week 2. The opener and the reason for the call.**
Practise nothing but the first thirty seconds. Name, company, an honest acknowledgment that this is a cold call, and one specific reason for calling. Twenty runs a day against a friendly prospect, then twenty against one who is irritated. They need to get through the first thirty seconds without apologising, rambling or asking "is now a bad time?". [Gong's data](https://www.gong.io/cold-calling) on 90,380 calls had "did I catch you at a bad time?" booking at 0.9%, 40% below baseline, while stating "the reason for my call" raised success 2.1 times. Our post on [cold call openers](/blog/best-cold-call-openers) covers what to say instead.

**Week 3. Objections and discovery.**
Take the five objections your team hears most and drill each one until the rep can acknowledge, isolate and redirect it without looking at notes. Then add two discovery questions the rep can ask once the prospect has engaged. Two, not eight. A cold call is not a discovery call.

**Week 4. Full calls, graded.**
The rep runs full cold calls against a resistant prospect, gets a transcript and a score on each, and reviews the lowest-scoring dimension with you once a day for fifteen minutes. By the end of the week they should be getting a next step from a resistant prospect roughly one call in five. That is not a real conversion rate. It is a sign they can hold the shape of a call under pressure.

What to measure in month one: calls practised (aim for over a hundred), the score on each of the six dimensions (opener, reason for call, discovery, objection handling, value, close) and, most usefully, the trend. A rep whose objection-handling score is climbing is ready. A rep whose scores are flat after eighty calls needs a different kind of help, and month one is the time to find that out.

## Days 31 to 60: real dials, tight loop

The rep goes live on a small, low-stakes segment of the list: a territory that is fine to warm up on, or closed-lost accounts from two years ago.

Set the activity target low and the review cadence high. Forty dials a day is plenty when every call is being listened to. The manager reviews three calls a day: one that went well, one that went badly, and one the rep picks. Fifteen minutes, one dimension, one thing to change tomorrow.

Keep practice running alongside. When a real call goes wrong on a specific objection, the rep runs that objection five times in practice the same afternoon, then reads the transcripts. This is where practice pays off most, because there is a real failure to attach it to. It is also why a single training day does not stick. [A 2015 replication of the forgetting curve](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0120644) found retention of new material falls to about 32% after a day and 4% after a month without reinforcement.

What to measure in month two: connect rate (so you can separate list problems from rep problems), conversations over two minutes as a share of connects, meetings booked, and the objection-handling score on practice calls. The meetings number will be small and noisy. Conversations over two minutes is the one that tells you whether the first thirty seconds are working. [Chorus found](http://blog.chorus.ai/the-secret-to-a-scheduled-meeting-longer-cold-calls) only 10% of connected cold calls pass two minutes and the ones that booked averaged 7.5 minutes.

## Days 61 to 90: production, with a number

Move the rep onto the real list and give them a meetings target that is a fraction of a tenured rep's, rising each week. Reviews drop to two or three a week. Practice continues, but the rep chooses what to drill from their own transcripts.

This is also where the rep takes over their own coaching. Have them pick their weakest dimension each Monday, name the change they are making, and show you a transcript on Friday where it worked. If they can do that, they are through onboarding whatever the calendar says.

What to measure in month three: meetings booked, meetings held (a rep whose meetings do not happen has a close problem, not a pipeline problem), and the one dimension score they said they were working on.

## The manager's part

The plan asks a manager for about fifteen minutes a day in months one and two. Most managers do not have it. The [Sales Management Association](https://salesmanagement.org/web/uploads/pdf-renamed-by-uzzal/89f5f8ff556f7a60adf7f1a78eac94c1.pdf) measured about 36 minutes a week of coaching per rep, and 77% of managers think their reps get too little. So onboarding drifts into "listen to some calls and we'll catch up Friday".

Two things make it fit. First, a fixed rubric. If every call is scored on the same six things, a review is a conversation about one number that moved, not a re-listen from the top. Second, let the rep do the volume without you. A hundred practice calls in month one is not a hundred calls you have to hear. You need the transcripts of the three that matter. The [coaching view](/for-managers) in 100 Dials exists for exactly that: skills by rep, the objections the team mishandles, and the calls underneath. [The same association](https://salesmanagement.org/blog/sales-coaching-your-greatest-lever-on-quota/) found coaching reps on activities, the calls themselves, lifted productivity 24%, against 10% for coaching on results.

<Practise>
Set a new rep up with three personas from your real target accounts and have them run twenty Level 1 calls in their first week, doing nothing but the opener and the reason for calling. Move to Level 3 in week two. Compare their opener and reason-for-call scores at the end of each week. Both should be up before they touch the dialler.
</Practise>
