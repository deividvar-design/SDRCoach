An SDR should talk slightly more than half the time on a cold call. Gong's analysis of 90,380 cold calls found that [reps talked a little over half the time on calls that booked a meeting](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats), the reverse of the discovery-call guidance most managers coach to. In our calls that lands between 40 and 60 percent over the whole call and close to 80 percent in the first minute, because the rep has to say who they are and why they are calling before the prospect has any reason to speak. The ratio itself is a weak coaching target. The length of the rep's longest unbroken turn, and what the prospect does straight after it, tell you far more about whether the call is going anywhere.

Every conversation-intelligence tool reports talk ratio, and most managers coach to a number borrowed from discovery calls. Applying it to cold calls produces reps who ask a stranger "what keeps you up at night?" fifteen seconds into an interruption.

## Cold calls are a pitch that becomes a conversation

Gong's successful cold calls had a distinct shape. They begin with the rep talking and turn into a dialogue as the prospect engages. [Successful cold calls averaged 5 minutes 50 seconds and unsuccessful ones 3 minutes 14 seconds](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats). The extra two and a half minutes were made of back-and-forth, not of longer pitches.

Two other platforms found the same shape. Chorus measured the [average connected cold call at about 80 seconds, with only 10% passing two minutes, while calls that booked averaged 7.5 minutes](http://blog.chorus.ai/the-secret-to-a-scheduled-meeting-longer-cold-calls). Orum put the [typical cold call at about 52 seconds and the calls that book at about six minutes](https://www.orum.com/blog/how-long-is-a-cold-call). Length is a result of the prospect deciding to engage, not a target to pad towards.

## The number that matters more than the ratio

A rep can hit 50 percent talk time two ways: twelve short turns, or one long pitch followed by a long stretch of the prospect explaining why not. The ratio is identical. The calls are not.

[The rep's longest monologue was 37 seconds in successful calls and 25 seconds in unsuccessful ones](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats). On a cold call the one long turn, the opener plus the reason for the call delivered without interruption, correlates with success. The rep who got 37 seconds got to finish the reason for the call. The rep who got 25 was cut off at "we're a".

The matching figure on the prospect's side is the one to watch. [The prospect's average monologue was 3.5 seconds in successful calls and 8 seconds in unsuccessful ones](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats). A prospect who talks at length early in a cold call is not opening up. They are explaining why they are about to hang up.

So the coaching rule, from our calls rather than from Gong, is this. One long turn is allowed, and it is the reason for the call. After that, no single rep turn over thirty seconds until the prospect has asked you a question. If you need more than thirty seconds to explain what you do, the explanation is the problem. We call the failure the second pitch: the prospect says something non-committal and the rep fills the gap with a longer version of the first turn.

## Cold call targets against discovery call targets

| Metric | Cold call target | Discovery call target |
|---|---|---|
| Rep talk ratio | 40 to 60 percent over the call, near 80 in the first minute. Gong found reps talk slightly more than half the time on successful calls | Around 30 percent. The prospect carries the call |
| Longest rep monologue | One long turn for the reason for the call. Gong measured 37 seconds on successful calls. Every turn after it under 30 seconds | Short throughout. The rep's job is the next question |
| Questions asked | Two to four in a five-minute call, in our observation. Gong found no significant difference between successful and unsuccessful cold calls | The main metric. Open, problem-focused, following up on the last answer |
| Prospect's average turn | Short early. Gong measured 3.5 seconds on successful calls and 8 on unsuccessful. A long early turn is usually an objection | Long. The prospect explaining their process is the point |
| Call length | 5 minutes 50 seconds for successful calls, 3 minutes 14 seconds for unsuccessful. A result, not a target | Set by the calendar, so not a signal |

The cold-call column says which figures are Gong's and which are our observation. Every row flips.

## A worked exchange

Here is 50 percent talk time done badly. The rep's first turn, trimmed here, ran well past a minute.

> Rep: Hi Mark, it's Dana at Fleetwise. The reason I'm calling is we help logistics firms like yours with driver hours and compliance. We've got a platform that integrates with your telematics, pulls the tachograph data automatically, flags infringements before they happen, and we also do route optimisation, so I wanted to see if it'd make sense to...
>
> Prospect: We've got something for that. Not interested, thanks.
>
> Rep: Oh, OK. Can I ask what you're using?
>
> Prospect: I'd rather not. I'm in the middle of something. Bye.

The ratio on that call is near 50 percent, because the prospect's exit took a while. The longest monologue is the whole first turn. The number of questions is one, asked after the call was already over.

Here is the same prospect, and roughly the same ratio, with short turns.

> Rep: Mark, it's Dana at Fleetwise. The reason I'm calling is you took on twelve new drivers in September, and the two firms we work with who did that both had an infringement spike in the first quarter. Fair to take thirty seconds?
>
> Prospect: Go on.
>
> Rep: When the new drivers started, who ended up checking their hours? The ops team, or did it land on you?
>
> Prospect: Me, mostly. We've got a system but nobody's set the alerts up.
>
> Rep: So the data's there and nobody's looking at it. How long has that been the case?
>
> Prospect: Since the summer, honestly.

Same rep, same prospect, same ratio. The longest monologue is the reason for the call, under thirty seconds. The prospect's turns are short and they are answers, not objections. By the third exchange the prospect has described a problem out loud, which the long pitch made impossible.

## What to do with the number

Do not coach the ratio. Coach the turn. Pull up the longest monologue on the transcript, read it aloud with the rep, and cut it in half together. Then have them run the same call again. The ratio takes care of itself once the turns are short.

The second thing to coach is the question count, usually downwards. Gong found [no significant difference in questions asked between successful and unsuccessful cold calls](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-stats). On a cold call, two to four questions in five minutes is enough in our experience. Fewer means the rep pitched. More means the rep ran discovery on someone who had not agreed to it. The third is time to first objection. Early is fine. What matters is whether the rep acknowledged it before answering.

Those four numbers are measurements rather than judgements, and they belong next to the scorecard rather than on it.
