A cold call scorecard needs six skills, a weight for each, and a written anchor for what a 3, a 5 and an 8 sound like. The six are the opener, the reason for the call, discovery, objection handling, the value proposition and the close. Weight them toward what books meetings: reason for the call, objection handling and the close carry 20 percent each, the opener and discovery 15 percent each, and the value proposition 10 percent. Score from the transcript, quote the line, and let the prospect decide the outcome. This is the rubric we grade every practice call against, published in full.

## Why most scorecards do not survive a second manager

The usual cold call scorecard has twelve to twenty rows. Some are skills and some are moods: "confidence", "energy", "rapport". A manager ticks the rows on feel and gets a number that looks precise and is not. Hand the same recording to a second manager and it moves by two or three points. Hand it back to the first manager on Friday and it moves anyway.

The fix is fewer rows, each defined tightly enough that two people hearing the same call land within a point of each other. Every row has to be something the rep either said or did not say. "Stated the reason for the call in the first thirty seconds, specific to the prospect's role" is scoreable. "Built rapport" is not, because nobody agrees what it sounds like.

## The six skills

Print the definition next to the score. A number without one is a mood with a decimal point.

**Opener.** The first fifteen seconds. Confident and clear, states name and company, uses a proven line such as a permission-based or pattern-interrupt opener rather than "did I catch you at a bad time?" or "how are you today?". Sets a calm, unhurried tone. Gong's analysis of hundreds of millions of calls found the polite check-in openers get reps hung up on, which is why they cost points here. The [best and worst openers](/blog/best-cold-call-openers) post has the detail.

**Reason for call.** States the reason for calling explicitly and early, in words close to "the reason for my call is", and makes it specific to this prospect's role, company or a trigger event rather than a generic pitch. Earns the right to continue. New reps most often score a 2 or 3 here.

**Discovery.** Asks problem-focused, open questions tied to the prospect's world. Follows up on what the prospect actually said. Avoids interrogation, and avoids turning a cold call into a full discovery meeting. Two good questions beat six.

**Objection handling.** Acknowledges each objection before responding, stays composed, isolates the real concern, reframes with relevance, and redirects to a question or a next step instead of arguing or caving. Score the worst-handled objection on the call, not the average.

**Value proposition.** Connects what the company does to a problem the prospect revealed. Outcome-led, concrete, brief, no feature dumps, with proof such as a peer or a number where it fits. On a cold call this is one or two sentences. A ninety-second pitch fails this row however good it was.

**Close.** Asks for a specific, small next step, usually a fifteen to thirty minute call, with a proposed day and time. Handles a soft no, confirms the details, and ends cleanly. "Would it make sense to chat sometime?" is not a close. "Are you free Thursday at ten for twenty minutes?" is.

## The weights, and why the close beats the opener

| Skill | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | 15% | Keeps the call alive, books nothing on its own |
| Reason for call | 20% | Best predictor that the prospect stays on the line |
| Discovery | 15% | Two questions is enough on a cold call |
| Objection handling | 20% | Most calls die at the first objection |
| Value proposition | 10% | Two sentences, and reps overweight it already |
| Close | 20% | No ask, no meeting. The most-skipped step |

The overall is the weighted sum, rounded to one decimal place. Three skills carry 60 percent between them: reason for the call, objection handling and the close. Those are where a cold call is won or lost, and the three reps most often skip, rush or fold on. Two reps show why.

| Skill | Rep A | Rep B |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | 9 | 5 |
| Reason for call | 4 | 8 |
| Discovery | 9 | 5 |
| Objection handling | 4 | 7 |
| Value proposition | 9 | 4 |
| Close | 3 | 8 |
| Straight average | 6.3 | 6.2 |
| Weighted overall | 5.8 | 6.5 |

Rep A sounds great, and never said why they were calling, went to features when pushed back, and ended with "I'll send some info over". Rep B is rougher and books the meeting. The weighted card gets it right.

## Anchors: what a 3, a 5 and an 8 sound like

Without anchors a 7 means "quite good" and every manager has a different quite. These are ours, drawn from grading practice calls rather than any industry standard. Tune the wording to your market once you have scored fifty calls with them.

| Skill | A 3 sounds like | A 5 sounds like | An 8 sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opener | "Hi, is this a bad time?" then a rushed company name | Name and company clear, then straight into the pitch | Name, company, a permission line, and a half-second pause the prospect can breathe in |
| Reason for call | Never stated. Pitch starts at second ten | "I'm calling about our platform" | "The reason for my call is you've just opened a second warehouse, and that usually means..." |
| Discovery | No questions, or "does that make sense?" | One open question, answer not followed up | Two questions, the second built on the prospect's answer |
| Objection handling | Argues, or caves and offers to email | Acknowledges, then repeats the pitch | Acknowledges, asks one question that isolates the concern, redirects to a next step |
| Value proposition | Feature list, forty seconds or more | Generic benefit not tied to anything the prospect said | One outcome tied to the problem the prospect named, with a peer or a number |
| Close | "I'll send something over" | "Would you be open to a call sometime?" | "Twenty minutes Thursday at ten?", handles the soft no, confirms |

A 0 to 2 means the skill did not happen or damaged the call. A 9 or 10 should be rare enough that a rep who gets one remembers it. If your team's average sits above 8, the scale has drifted. Re-read the last ten 8s against the anchor.

## Rules that keep the scores honest

**Score from the transcript, not from memory.** If you cannot point at the line, it does not go on the card. Every score gets one sentence of evidence quoting the rep. "Close, 3: ended with 'I'll drop you an email with some times' and hung up without a date." That sentence is the coaching. The number is the filing system.

**The prospect decides the outcome.** The scorecard grades the skills. Whether the meeting was booked, a callback agreed, information requested or the call rejected is a separate field, judged from the prospect's own closing words. The rep does not fill it in. Straight 7s with no meeting is a real question to answer.

**Add the numbers the scorer cannot fudge.** Alongside the six scores, record talk ratio, longest monologue, questions asked, filler words and time to first objection. They are counted, not judged, so they anchor the judgement. A discovery score of 7 next to "questions asked: 1" needs a second look. Our [talk ratio](/blog/cold-call-talk-ratio) post covers what those numbers should be on a cold call.

**Track the dimension, not the call.** One bad close is a Tuesday. Five calls in a row with the lowest score on close is a coaching plan. Keep a running average per skill per rep and let the weakest skill pick the next drill. Three consecutive calls averaging 7 or better at one prospect difficulty means the rep is ready for a harder one, as a recommendation rather than a gate.

## Using the card across a team

One rubric, applied the same way to every rep, turns a scorecard into a team measurement. Grade every call on the same six rows with the same anchors and you can see that the team's weakest dimension is the close, that three reps fold on "send me an email" and one does not, and that the new starter's reason for call moved from 3 to 6 in a fortnight.

If managers do the grading, the anchors above and the evidence rule will get two of them within a point most of the time. If software does it, the same rubric should be applied with a rationale quoting the rep's own words, which is how 100 Dials grades practice calls and how the [enablement view](/for-enablement) rolls the scores into a heatmap per rep and per dimension. Either way, the rubric is the asset. Whoever applies it should be replaceable without the scores changing.

<Practise>
Pick your weakest of the six skills from your last five scored calls. Run five calls against a Level 2 prospect changing only that skill, then score each transcript against the anchor table before you look at the coach's score. If you are more than a point apart, discuss that anchor with your manager.
</Practise>
