A cold call scorecard should grade six skills and nothing else: the opener, the reason for the call, discovery, objection handling, the value proposition and the close. Each skill gets a one-line definition, a score from 0 to 10, and a written anchor for what a 2, a 5, a 7 and a 9 sound like. The weights are uneven on purpose. The reason for the call, objection handling and the close carry 20 percent each, because they separate a booked meeting from a polite hang-up. The opener and discovery carry 15 percent. The value proposition carries 10. The full card is below, with the anchors, so two managers scoring the same call land within a point of each other.

## Why most scorecards do not survive contact with a real call

The scorecard in most enablement decks has three problems, and any one of them makes the scores useless for coaching.

**It grades things nobody can point to.** "Confidence", "energy", "rapport". A manager cannot quote the line where the rep lost confidence, so the score becomes a mood. Two managers watching the same call give a 6 and an 8 and both are right, because there is nothing to be right about.

**It has too many rows.** Twelve dimensions feels thorough. In practice the manager runs out of attention around row seven, the last rows get a reflexive 7, and the rep gets twelve things to fix and remembers none. Six is the most a manager can score honestly from one listen.

**It has no anchors.** A 7 for one manager is a 5 for another. Without a sentence saying what a 5 looks like, the scale drifts with whoever is scoring. The anchors are the scorecard. The headings are just labels.

## The six skills and their weights

| Skill | What it covers | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | The first fifteen seconds. Name, company, a calm tone, a proven line. | 15% |
| Reason for call | Stated early and explicitly, specific to this prospect's role, company or trigger event. | 20% |
| Discovery | Open, problem-focused questions tied to the prospect's world. Follows up on what they said. | 15% |
| Objection handling | Acknowledges, isolates the real concern, reframes, redirects to a question. No arguing, no caving. | 20% |
| Value proposition | Connects the offer to a problem the prospect revealed. Outcome-led, concrete, brief. | 10% |
| Close | Asks for a specific small next step with a proposed day and time, handles a soft no, confirms. | 20% |

These are the six dimensions in the 100 Dials rubric, with the weights the grader applies. The definitions lean on published cold-call research, in particular Gong's analysis of several hundred million calls, which is where the evidence against "did I catch you at a bad time?" and for stating the reason for the call comes from. The exact percentages are our choice, not a published figure. Treat them as a defensible starting point.

## Why the weights are uneven

An equal-weight scorecard tells reps that a great opener is worth as much as a close. It is not. A call with a brilliant opener and no ask books nothing. A plain opener, a specific reason and a clear ask with a proposed time books at a normal rate.

Two reps make the point. The first opens and pitches well but never asks: opener 8, reason for call 6, discovery 6, objection handling 5, value proposition 8, close 2. The second is ordinary at the start, specific about why they are calling, handles the pushback and asks: opener 5, reason for call 8, discovery 5, objection handling 7, value proposition 5, close 8.

| | Rep one | Rep two |
|---|---|---|
| Simple average | 5.8 | 6.3 |
| Weighted overall | 5.5 | 6.6 |

The simple average puts them half a point apart. The weighted score puts them more than a point apart, in the right direction. Rep two books meetings. Rep one gets compliments.

The value proposition sits at 10 percent because on a cold call the value line is short, comes late, and only works if discovery surfaced a problem to attach it to. A rep who scores well on discovery usually scores well on value, so more weight would double count. Discovery sits at 15 because a cold call is not a discovery meeting. Two sharp questions are the target.

## The anchors: what a 2, a 5, a 7 and a 9 sound like

Calibrate the scale before anyone scores a call. Ours is that 5 is an average new SDR, 7 is a solid rep who would book at a normal rate, and 9 or above is rare and means the skill was done near-perfectly on this call. A skill the rep never attempted is a 1, not a 5.

| Skill | 2 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opener | "How are you today?" or "bad time?", rushed, name or company missing | Name and company stated, fine tone, a default line | Calm and unhurried, a permission-based or pattern-interrupt line, the prospect lets them continue | All of that, and the prospect's first reply is a question, not "who is this?" |
| Reason for call | Never stated, or a generic pitch ("we help companies like yours") | Stated, but it could be read to any name on the list | Stated early and explicitly, specific to the role, company or a trigger event | One sentence, specific, and it earns a "go on" from a cold prospect |
| Discovery | No questions, or a string of yes or no questions | Open questions from a list, no follow-up on the answer | Problem-focused questions tied to the prospect's world, builds on what they said | Two or three sharp questions that surface a real problem, no checklist |
| Objection handling | Argues, or caves at the first push ("OK, I'll send an email") | Acknowledges, then repeats the pitch | Acknowledges, isolates the real concern, reframes, redirects to a question | Does that on every objection, composed throughout, and the resistance visibly drops |
| Value proposition | A feature list, or no value statement at all | Outcome-led but generic, not tied to anything the prospect said | Connects the offer to a problem the prospect revealed, brief and concrete | One line, tied to the revealed problem, with a peer or a number as proof |
| Close | No ask, or "can I send you some information?" | Asks for a meeting with no day or time, or accepts a vague "maybe" | Asks for a specific small step with a proposed day and time, handles one soft no | That, plus confirms the details and ends the call cleanly |

Print this table. When a manager hesitates between a 6 and a 7, the question is not "how did it feel" but "which column does the transcript match".

## How to score a call with it

**Score from the transcript, not from memory.** Listening live produces an impression. Reading the transcript with the card open produces evidence. Every score should come with one quoted line from the rep, or a note that the line never came.

**Grade the rep, not the prospect.** A hostile prospect does not lower the rep's scores. A rep who stayed composed, acknowledged three objections and asked for the meeting has done the job even if the answer was no.

**Keep the outcome separate.** Booked, callback, information requested, rejected or hung up is a fact about the call, decided by the prospect's final words. It is not a seventh row on the card. A rep can score 7 across the board and still get a no on a given dial. Over twenty dials the scores and the outcomes converge.

**Keep the stats separate too.** Talk ratio, longest monologue, questions asked and filler words are measurements, not judgements. They belong next to the card, not on it, and they cannot be argued with. A [talk ratio that drifts past seventy percent](/blog/cold-call-talk-ratio) explains a low discovery score better than any adjective. The [one-rubric approach on our enablement page](/for-enablement) pairs the six scores with those stats for this reason.

**Coach the lowest row.** The weighted overall is for tracking a rep over time. The coaching conversation is about one number: the lowest dimension on the card. Pick it, drill it for a week, score again. A rep who moves their close from 3 to 6 improves their overall by 0.6 without touching anything else, and learns one thing properly instead of six things badly.

**Use a threshold for progression.** We recommend a harder practice level after three calls averaging 7 or better at the current one. Without a threshold, progression is a manager's whim, and reps know it.

## What to leave off the card

Anything that cannot be matched to a column in the anchor table. "Confidence" is a symptom of a good opener and a stated reason, so score those. "Product knowledge" belongs in a quiz. "Energy" usually means the manager liked the rep.

Resist adding a row for every new thing the team gets wrong. If reps are mishandling "send me an email", that is an objection handling problem. Tag it and drill it. The card has to stay the same for scores to be comparable in March and in September. The moment the rows change, every trend line resets.
