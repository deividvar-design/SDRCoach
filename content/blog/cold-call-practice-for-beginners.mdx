If you are new to cold calling, your first ten practice calls should cover the first thirty seconds and nothing else. Your name, your company, an honest reason for calling, then one objection. Hang up after that. Do not try a full call, do not practise discovery questions, do not polish the pitch. Most cold calls are lost before any of that matters, and the beginner's mistakes all live in the opening. Get the first thirty seconds to the point where you can say them without reading, then add the next piece. This post is what to drill, what to leave alone for now, and how to score yourself honestly.

## Why the first thirty seconds are the whole job at first

A cold call is an interruption. The prospect was doing something else, the phone rang, and a stranger is talking. In the first few seconds they are deciding one thing: is this worth another thirty seconds. Everything a beginner is usually taught, the questions, the value statement, the proof point, comes after that decision. If the decision goes against you, none of it is heard.

So the opening is not a warm-up to the call. It is the call. Gong Labs, which analysed hundreds of millions of cold calls, found that the opening line alone moved the booking rate several times over, before any pitch was heard. Beginners who spend their first week rehearsing the pitch are rehearsing the part of the call they will rarely reach.

## What to drill in your first ten calls

**Your name and company, said slowly.** Nervous reps rush the one line the prospect needs. Say your full name, then your company, with a pause between. The prospect should be able to repeat both back. If you are practising against a colleague, ask them to.

**An honest acknowledgement that this is a cold call.** Not an apology. "I know I'm calling out of the blue" or "you weren't expecting this call" said plainly, once. The prospect already knows. Saying it first tells them you are not going to pretend otherwise, and most people relax a little when the obvious thing is named.

**One specific reason for calling.** This is the line to spend most of your practice on. "The reason for my call is" followed by something that is true about this prospect's role, company or situation. Not what your company does. Not "to introduce myself". Gong Labs found reps who stated their reason explicitly booked meetings at roughly double the rate of those who did not. We have a full post on [building the reason for the call](/blog/reason-for-call), with examples. For now, write three reasons for three different kinds of prospect, and say each one out loud until it sounds like you.

**One objection.** Pick the one you dread most. For most beginners it is "not interested" or "send me an email". Your job is not to overcome it in the first ten calls. Your job is to acknowledge it before you respond, in one short sentence, and then ask one question. "Fair enough. Can I ask what you're using at the moment?" That is the whole move. Winning the objection comes later. Not panicking at it comes now.

## What to ignore for now

**Discovery depth.** You will be told to ask open questions about the prospect's problems. You will. Not yet. A beginner asking "what keeps you up at night" twenty seconds into an interruption sounds like a script, because it is one. Two questions is plenty on a cold call, and you earn the right to ask them with the reason for calling, which is what you are practising.

**Value proposition polish.** The paragraph about what your product does and why it is better is the part beginners rehearse most and reach least. Leave it as a single plain sentence for now. You will refine it once you have heard what prospects actually say, which you cannot do until you survive the opening.

**The close.** You should know the ask: a short call, a proposed day and time. But in the first ten practice calls you are hanging up after the first objection, so the close is not on the table. Add it in the second ten.

## Three mistakes beginners make in the first thirty seconds

**Opening with "how are you today" or "did I catch you at a bad time".** Both sound polite and both cost you the call. The first is what every cold caller says, and the prospect's guard goes up on hearing it. The second gives the prospect a one-word exit, and Gong Labs found it was the worst-performing opener in its data, roughly forty percent less likely to book a meeting than the baseline. Our breakdown of [the best and worst cold call openers](/blog/best-cold-call-openers) goes through the alternatives. Pick one and use it for all ten calls so you can hear how it lands.

**Pitching before stating a reason.** The beginner hears silence after their name, panics, and fills it with what the company does. The prospect hears a pitch from a stranger and reaches for the brush-off. Reverse the order. Reason first, in one sentence that is about them. The pitch, if it ever comes, comes after they have engaged.

**Talking faster after an objection.** The prospect says "we're not interested" and the beginner's voice rises half a tone and speeds up. The prospect hears nerves and knows the call is theirs. Gong Labs found top performers in sales conversations do the opposite: they pause for over a second before answering an objection and hold their pace, while average reps speed up. In practice, when you hear the objection, breathe once before you speak. It will feel like a long silence. It is not.

## A self-check for your first ten calls

Score each practice call yourself, out of ten, on the six skills a good cold call scorecard uses. For the first ten calls, only the first two rows and the fourth matter. The others are there so you can see what is coming.

| Skill | What a 3 sounds like | What a 7 sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Opener | Rushed name, "how are you today", or an apology | Full name and company, a plain acknowledgement, unhurried |
| Reason for call | A company description, or "to introduce myself" | One sentence that is specific to this prospect's role or company |
| Discovery | Not relevant yet | Not relevant yet |
| Objection handling | Argued, defended, or caved straight away | Acknowledged in one sentence, asked one question, held pace |
| Value proposition | Not relevant yet | Not relevant yet |
| Close | Not relevant yet | Not relevant yet |

Be stingy with yourself. A seven means you would be happy for your manager to hear it. If you give yourself a seven on every call, the scale is telling you nothing. The useful signal is the trend: by call ten, are the opener and reason for call higher than they were on call one. If they are, add the second objection and the close. If they are not, listen back to the recordings and find the line that keeps going wrong. It is usually the same line.

## Who to practise against

A colleague is better than a mirror and worse than you think. Colleagues know the pitch and go easy, and after two runs the exercise becomes a chat. Ask them to play a specific prospect, give them two objections to raise, and tell them not to agree to anything. Keep each run under a minute.

A recording of a real call is useful for hearing how prospects actually talk, less useful for practising, because you cannot reply. An AI prospect built from real target accounts gives you the repetitions nobody else has time for: twenty runs of the same thirty seconds in a morning, scored the same way each time, with nobody watching you fail. If your manager runs a practice programme, they can see the trend across the team, which is what the [page for sales managers](/for-managers) describes. If they do not, the self-check table above and twenty minutes a day will get you most of the way.

Whatever you practise against, keep the runs short and the count high. Ten full calls teach you less than forty openings. The forty openings are what make the eleventh full call go differently.
