There are only so many ways to get a stranger off the phone, and prospects use the same eight over and over. That is good news. An objection you have heard four hundred times is not an objection, it is a cue, and a cue can be practised until the answer comes out before the nerves do.

Gong's analysis of cold-call objections found what most reps know from experience: the first objection is rarely the real one. It is a reflex, offered because the prospect was interrupted. What matters is what the rep does in the three seconds after it.

## The move, once

Every response below uses the same four-part shape, so learn it once.

Acknowledge: one short line that shows you heard them and are not going to argue. Isolate: a question that finds out whether this objection is the real one. Reframe: one sentence that puts it in a different light. Redirect: back to the reason for the call, or to a small next step.

You will not always use all four. But if you skip the first you sound like a telemarketer, and if you skip the second you spend the call answering an objection they did not mean.

## 1. "I don't have time right now"

What it usually means: you interrupted them, and they want to know how long this is going to take. It is rarely about the calendar.

Accept it and set a boundary you can keep. "Completely fair, you weren't expecting me. Give me thirty seconds to say why I called, and if it's not useful, hang up on me. Deal?" Then use thirty seconds, not ninety. If they say no even to that, ask for a time: "When's less bad, later today or tomorrow morning?" A specific slot is a yes-or-no question.

## 2. "I'm not interested"

What it usually means: you have not yet said anything worth being interested in. On a cold call this usually arrives before the reason for calling, so it is a reaction to being sold to, not to what you sell.

Acknowledge, then get specific. "That's fair, I haven't told you anything yet. The reason I called is that you added two depots this year, and most ops leads I speak to at that stage are fighting with driver scheduling. Is that on your list, or is that not you?" You have replaced a generic pitch with a specific claim about their world and given them an easy way to correct you. Being corrected is a conversation.

If it comes after the reason for calling, isolate it. "Not interested in fixing it, or not interested in talking to a vendor about it?" Those are very different answers.

## 3. "Just send me an email"

What it usually means: a polite no. The prospect expects you to say yes, ask for the address and go away.

Agree, then ask what to put in it. "Happy to. So it's not something you delete, what's the one thing you'd want it to answer?" Whatever they say, answer it in one sentence on the call, then offer a fifteen-minute slot. We have a [full post on this one](/blog/handling-send-me-an-email), because it is the objection reps fold on most.

## 4. "We already have a solution for that"

What it usually means: they have something in place and do not want the disruption of changing it. This is the strongest of the eight, because it is often true.

Do not attack the incumbent. Ask about it. "Good, that means you've already decided this matters. How long have you had it?" and then "What's the one thing you'd change about it if you could?" Most people have an answer. That answer is your reason for a next step: "That's actually the thing we're usually brought in for. Worth fifteen minutes to see if it's the same problem?" If they say nothing needs changing, believe them, ask when the renewal is, and put a note in the diary.

## 5. "There's no budget for this"

What it usually means: one of two things. Either there is no budget because nobody has made the case yet, or there is no budget because they are not the person who holds it. Find out which.

"Understood. Is that no budget this year, or no budget until someone shows it pays for itself?" If the first, ask when planning happens and aim for that. If the second, that is what the meeting is for: "That's the conversation I'd want to have, because most teams your size find it in the overtime line. Fifteen minutes to see if that's true for you?"

Never respond with a price. Budget is not a price question, it is a priority question.

## 6. "I'm not the right person"

What it usually means: sometimes exactly that. Sometimes it means "I am the right person and I would like you to go away".

Take it at face value and ask for the referral, with a specific role. "Thanks for saying so. Who owns fleet scheduling at the moment, is that Ops or does it sit with Finance?" Naming a role is easier to answer than "who should I talk to?". Then, before you go: "Out of interest, what's your involvement in it?" A surprising number of wrong people turn out to be the person who feels the problem, even if they do not sign for it.

## 7. "Call me back later"

What it usually means: a delay they hope you will forget. Occasionally it is genuine, in which case they will give you a time.

Accept it and make it concrete. "Will do. Is later this week or next better?" then "Tuesday at ten, or Thursday at two?" If they refuse to give a time, it was a no. Treat it as "not interested" and ask one specific question about the reason for calling before you let them go.

When you do call back, open with it: "You asked me to call back Thursday, so this is that call." It is the easiest opener you will ever use.

## 8. "How did you get my number?"

What it usually means: they are annoyed, and they are testing whether you will be straight with them.

Be straight. "It's on your company's site", or "I found it through a data provider we pay for, which I know isn't a great answer." Then do not linger. "The reason I'm calling is..." and go. A defensive or vague answer confirms you are the kind of caller they were worried about. An honest one, delivered without apology, usually resets the call.

## Practise the reflex, not the script

Read these eight aloud and they sound reasonable. The problem is that on a real call the objection arrives while you are still mid-sentence, from someone who sounds irritated, and the polished answer is replaced by "okay, no problem, sorry to bother you". The gap between knowing the words and saying them under pressure is the whole skill, and it only closes with repetition.
