To close a cold call, ask for a small next step at a time you choose: "Worth fifteen minutes to see if it fits? I have Thursday at ten or Friday at two." Then handle the hedge with one alternative slot rather than more pitch, and confirm the time, the length and the email address before you hang up, with the invite sent while the prospect is still on the line. The close fails in three places: reps never ask, they ask without a time, or they get a yes and let it go cold. A proposed time fixes the first two because it gives the prospect something concrete to accept or move. Confirming on the call fixes the third, because a meeting that is not in the calendar when the call ends is still a maybe.

## Where cold call closes actually break

Most coaching on the close is about nerve. Nerve is rarely the problem. Transcripts of calls that ended without a meeting show something more mechanical.

The first failure is no ask at all. The call ends on "thanks for your time" because the rep was waiting for the prospect to suggest a next step. Prospects did not ask for this call and will not plan the follow-up for you.

The second is the ask with no shape. "Would you be open to chatting sometime?" gets a polite "sure, maybe" and the rep logs it as a win. It is not one. There is no day, no length and no invite, so the prospect has agreed to nothing they can be held to. Two emails later it has gone quiet.

The third is the soft yes that leaks. The prospect says "Thursday's fine", the rep says "great, I'll send something over", and the invite arrives an hour later to a prospect who has moved on. In our observation a fair number of those invites never get accepted. The meeting was booked in the rep's head, not in anyone's calendar.

## The close, word for word

The close is four moves, in order, and each one has a job.

**1. Bridge from what they said.** Do not jump from a question to an ask. Tie the ask to the one thing the prospect told you. "You said the new reps are taking three months to get going. That is exactly what we look at."

**2. Ask for a small, specific step.** Name the length and name a time. "Worth fifteen minutes to see if it fits? I have Thursday at ten or Friday at two." Fifteen minutes is small enough to say yes to. Two named slots turn the question from "do you want a meeting" into "which time", and the second question is much easier to answer.

**3. Handle the hedge with a slot, not a pitch.** Do not sell harder. Most hedges here are about the diary, not the product. Offer one other slot, or shrink the ask. "If this week is bad, Tuesday at nine? And if it is not relevant you will know in five minutes."

**4. Confirm and send the invite on the call.** Read back the day, time and length. Check the email address. Ask if anyone else should be there. Then send it. "I am sending it now: fifteen minutes, Tuesday at nine. Can you see it?" The call ends with the meeting in their calendar, not on your to-do list.

Here is the whole thing in one piece.

> Rep: You said the new reps are taking about three months before they book anything. That is the part we work on. Worth fifteen minutes to see if it fits? I have Thursday at ten or Friday at two.
>
> Prospect: This week is a mess, to be honest.
>
> Rep: Fair. Tuesday at nine? If it is not relevant, you will know in five minutes.
>
> Prospect: Tuesday could work. Make it half nine.
>
> Rep: Tuesday at half nine, fifteen minutes. Is it still your first name at the company domain?
>
> Prospect: Yes.
>
> Rep: Anyone else who should see it, the person who runs onboarding?
>
> Prospect: Just me for now.
>
> Rep: Sending it now. It should land in the next few seconds.
>
> Prospect: Got it.
>
> Rep: Great. Talk Tuesday.

The rep never summarised the pitch again or said "I will send something over". Every line after the ask moved the meeting closer to the calendar.

## Strong close, weak close

The difference is usually visible in a single line. This is the comparison we use when reviewing transcripts.

| Moment | Weak version | Strong version | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| The ask | "Would you be open to a chat sometime?" | "Worth fifteen minutes? I have Thursday at ten or Friday at two." | A named slot is something to accept or move. "Sometime" is easy to agree to and never happens |
| The length | No length mentioned | "Fifteen minutes" | A prospect who does not know the cost of yes defaults to no |
| The hedge | Reopens the pitch with another feature | Offers one more slot or a smaller ask | The hedge is about the diary, not the product |
| "Send me something" | "Sure, what is the best email?" | "Happy to. What would it need to answer?" | The first ends the call. The second keeps it going |
| The confirm | "Great, I will send an invite" | Reads back day, time, length and email, sends while on the line | A meeting not in the calendar when the call ends is still a maybe |
| The exit | Recaps the pitch, thanks them twice | "Talk Tuesday." | Every extra sentence is a chance to reopen the decision |

The confirm row is the one most reps skip, because on a recording it sounds like admin. It is the difference between a booked meeting and a pending one.

## When the prospect says "send me something"

At the close, "send me something" is a soft no dressed as a request. Folding gets you an email nobody opens. Arguing gets you hung up on.

The response that works is short. Agree, ask what the email would need to answer, answer that in one sentence, and offer the slot again.

> Prospect: Just send me something and I will take a look.
>
> Rep: Happy to. So it is worth opening, what is the one thing it should answer?
>
> Prospect: Whether it works with the dialler we already use.
>
> Rep: It sits alongside it, nothing to replace. Easiest is fifteen minutes Thursday at ten and I will show you. Or Friday at two?

If they still decline, send exactly what they asked for, in three sentences, with a time to talk at the bottom. Then call again. We go through this objection step by step, including the branch where the prospect turns out not to be a prospect, in [how to handle "just send me an email" without sending the email](/blog/handling-send-me-an-email).

Two other hedges come up at the close often enough to prepare for. "Call me next quarter" is a timing objection, and the move is to pin a date before you hang up: "Happy to. First week of February, the Tuesday? I will put fifteen minutes in now and you can move it." A vague "later" is a no. "I need to check with my boss" is a reason to invite the boss, not to wait: "Makes sense. Would it help if they joined the fifteen minutes, so you do not have to relay it?"

## How managers should score the close

The close is the easiest skill to score honestly. A rep who had the chance to ask and never did has a close of 1 or 2, however good the rest of the call was. A rep who asked without a time sits in the middle. A rep who proposed a day and time and got it booked has done the core of the job.

That is how we score it. In the 100 Dials rubric the close carries 20 percent of the overall, and a meeting booked at a specific time the rep proposed scores at least a 6. The points above that come from how the rep locked it in: handling the back and forth on the slot, confirming the length and attendees, and ending cleanly. If the prospect offered the meeting first, or hung up before the rep had a turn to ask, the close is not marked down for that alone. The full anchors are in our [cold call scorecard template](/blog/cold-call-scorecard-template).

Sort the team by close score and listen for which failure each rep has. A rep at 2 never asks, and needs the ask drilled until it is automatic. A rep at 4 asks without a time, which is a one-sentence fix. A rep at 6 books but does not confirm.
