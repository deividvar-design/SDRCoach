Average SDR ramp time, measured as the gap between start date and the first month at full quota, is about three months on most teams. Ask ten managers and you will hear anything from six weeks to six months, and the spread is the point. Ramp is four separate learning curves, the buyer, the conversation, the list and the tooling, that most teams run at the same time on live prospects. The buyer and the list take roughly as long everywhere. The conversation is the part that varies between teams, and it is the part you can shorten, because it does not have to be learnt on your pipeline. The ranges in this post are what we see across the teams we talk to and work with, an observation rather than an industry benchmark.

## Three definitions of ramp, and why the quoted number moves

A manager who says their SDRs ramp in six weeks almost always means time to a first booked meeting. One who says six months usually means a rep hitting quota three months running. Neither is what finance means when it asks how long until the hire pays back.

| Definition | What it tells you | Typical range, our observation |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first booked meeting | The rep can hold a cold call to a next step | 2 to 4 weeks |
| Time to first full-quota month | The rep is productive | 10 to 14 weeks |
| Time to three consecutive quota months | The rep is reliable | 4 to 6 months |

Use the middle one. It is the first point at which the seat earns what it costs, and it is early enough to act on. Report the median rather than the mean, because one rep who takes seven months drags an average further than one who takes seven weeks lifts it.

Write the definition down before the next hire starts. A number that changes meaning between quarters cannot tell you whether onboarding is improving.

## Where the weeks go

A new SDR is learning four things at once, each with a different shape.

**The buyer.** Who you sell to, what they are measured on, and what goes wrong in their week that you fix. Two to three weeks on any team, and it does not compress much. It is reading, listening to recorded calls, and talking to people who already sell to these buyers.

**The list.** Which accounts and titles connect, which sequences get replies, what a good territory looks like. Three to four weeks, only learnt by working the list, and it overlaps with everything else.

**The tooling.** CRM, dialler, sequencer, the notes convention nobody wrote down. A week or two, and not where the time goes.

**The conversation.** The first thirty seconds, the reason for the call, what to do when the prospect says they already have something. This is the curve that varies most between teams, and the arithmetic explains why. A rep needs somewhere over a hundred real conversations before the opener stops sounding rehearsed and objections stop landing as surprises. On forty dials a day with a connect rate under ten percent, that is three or four conversations a day, so six weeks of calendar, every one of them a prospect from your list hearing a rep who does not yet know the job.

That six weeks is the gap between a ten-week ramp and a sixteen-week one. The other three curves are roughly fixed. This one is not, because a conversation can be practised on someone who is not a buyer.

## Ramp time by team size

The teams we talk to split into three groups. The differences are coaching capacity, not rep quality.

| Team | Typical ramp to full quota, our observation | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 SDRs, managed by a founder or an AE | 14 to 20 weeks | Nobody is listening to calls. The rep learns alone, from hang-ups, and the list is unproven. |
| 4 to 10 SDRs with a dedicated manager | 10 to 14 weeks | The manager has fifteen minutes a day per rep at best. Reviews happen, but late, on whichever calls were handy. |
| More than 10 SDRs with an enablement function | 8 to 12 weeks | A structured programme, peer roleplay, a call library. The risk is a programme that is a reading list while the conversation curve still runs on the pipeline. |

Two things stand out. The smallest teams ramp slowest, and not because they hire worse. A rep on a three-person team gets fewer reviewed calls in a month than a rep on a thirty-person team gets in a week, on a territory nobody has worked. And the largest teams still take two to three months with every advantage, because structure shortens the buyer and list curves but most programmes still run the first hundred conversations on live dials.

## The leading indicators that beat the calendar

Waiting three months to learn whether an onboarding plan worked is too slow to fix it for the rep inside it. Three signals tell you earlier.

**Conversations over two minutes, as a share of connects.** A connect that ends inside two minutes is a lost opener or a lost reason for the call. When this share climbs week on week, the first thirty seconds are working. Flat after three weeks of live dials means the rep is learning the wrong lesson from volume. Our post on [how much an SDR should talk](/blog/cold-call-talk-ratio) covers why the shape of those two minutes matters more than the count.

**Practice scores on a fixed rubric, in order.** The opener and the reason for the call move first, because they are the most mechanical. Objection handling moves next and tells you the most, because it only improves once a rep stops defending and starts asking. Score practice calls on the same six skills as real ones and you see this trend before the rep has burned a single account. On 100 Dials a rep is recommended up a level after three calls averaging 7 or better at their current level. That threshold, applied to the hardest level, is a reasonable definition of "ready to dial" that does not depend on the calendar.

**Meetings held, not meetings booked.** A rep who books meetings that do not happen has a close problem, not a pipeline problem. Held meetings lag by a week or two but predict a quota month.

Dials per day is not a leading indicator. It is the first number a new rep hits and says nothing about the conversation curve.

## How to shorten ramp without hurrying it

Shortening ramp does not mean putting a rep on the real list sooner. It means arriving there further along the conversation curve.

**Move the first hundred conversations into practice.** A hundred scored practice calls against a prospect who interrupts, asks who is calling and tries to hang up twice fit in the first two to three weeks, alongside the buyer work. Our [30-60-90 day onboarding plan](/blog/sdr-onboarding-plan-30-60-90) is built around this: month one is mostly practice, month two live dials with a tight review loop, month three production with a number. The rep goes live having already made their worst calls where nobody on your list could hear them.

**Give the rep a proven starter list.** Most of the variation in the list curve is a new rep being handed a territory nobody has worked. A segment a tenured rep has already connected into, or closed-lost accounts from two years ago, settles whether a quiet week is the list or the rep.

**Define ramped before day one, and tell the rep.** A rep who knows that ramped means a rising share of conversations over two minutes, three practice calls at 7 or better on the hardest level, and a first full-quota month will coach themselves toward it. A rep told to "get up to speed" will dial faster.

**Spend the manager's fifteen minutes on the right call.** The constraint on most teams is not knowing what to coach, it is the hour of listening it takes to find out. One rubric across every practice and live call makes a review a conversation about the one skill that moved, not a re-listen from the top. That is the case our [page for sales managers](/for-managers) makes.

Done this way, our observation is that the conversation curve goes from six weeks of live dials to two or three weeks of practice. A team at twelve weeks lands at nine or ten. A founder-led team at eighteen gets under fourteen, because the rep is no longer learning alone.

## What a ramp number is not for

Ramp time measures the onboarding programme, not the rep inside it. A rep at week fourteen with objection-handling scores still climbing and held meetings rising is on track. A rep at week eight with flat scores after a hundred practice calls needs a different conversation. The calendar cannot tell them apart. The leading indicators can.

Hold the team median against the plan, quarter on quarter, and each rep against their own trend. If the median has not moved after two cohorts, the plan is the problem, and the first place to look is how many of the first hundred conversations still happen on your pipeline.

<Practise>
Before a new rep's first live dial, have them run Level 1 calls until their opener and reason-for-call scores hold at 7 or better across three calls, then Level 3 until objection handling does the same. Note the date each threshold is hit. That is the rep's conversation curve, measured in days rather than in months of burned accounts.
</Practise>
