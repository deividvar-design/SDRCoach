There are three ways to train a cold call away from the live dialler: a colleague plays the prospect, the team reviews recordings of real calls, or the rep dials a simulator where software plays the prospect. They are not substitutes for each other. Roleplay is the only one where a human can stop the call and explain. Call review is the only one built on what real buyers actually said. A simulator is the only one that gives a rep fifty attempts at the same thirty seconds in a week, and repetition is the thing cold calling needs most and the other two cannot afford. The useful question is which job each one does.

## The three formats side by side

| | Peer or manager roleplay | Recorded-call review | AI cold call simulator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who plays the prospect | A colleague who knows the pitch | Nobody. The call already happened | Software, ideally built from real targets |
| Repetitions per rep per week | One or two | None. You watch, you do not dial | As many as the rep wants |
| Resistance | Depends on the colleague's mood | Real, but you cannot change your reply | Set by level, the same every time |
| Who decides the outcome | The person playing, usually kindly | The real buyer | The prospect, from what was said |
| Scoring | Impressions, varies by manager | Manager notes, if there is a rubric | The same rubric on every call |
| Manager time per rep per week | Twenty to thirty minutes | Fifteen minutes per call reviewed | A few minutes reading reports |
| Best at | The debrief and the why | Learning what your market says | Making the first thirty seconds automatic |

The time figures are our observation, not a survey. The shape is what matters: roleplay and review cost manager hours per repetition, and a simulator costs manager hours per report.

## What roleplay does that nothing else can

A human playing the prospect can stop. Halfway through an objection the manager can break character and say "that word, 'solution', is why I switched off, try it again". No recording can do that and no simulator can either, because it does not know what the rep was trying to do. Roleplay is the format for judgement: when to push, when to let a prospect go.

It is also the format with the worst ratio of cost to repetition. One rep, one manager, twenty minutes, perhaps three attempts. The rest of the team watches, which makes the rep perform rather than practise. We wrote about [how to run cold call roleplay reps do not hate](/blog/cold-call-roleplay), and the fixes all come down to the same thing: fewer people in the room, one skill per session, a prospect who can say no. Even fixed, it stays expensive. Spend it on the conversation about a call, not on the call.

## What call review does that nothing else can

Recorded-call review is the only format where the prospect is real. A rep hears a logistics VP say "we've already got a provider for that, and honestly it's fine" in the VP's own words, with the VP's own impatience. No roleplay partner produces that line unprompted and no generic simulator does either. Review is how a team learns what its market actually says, which objections come up in which order, and what the good reps did at the moment the call turned.

What it cannot do is let the listener try a different reply. The call is over. A rep can note "I should have asked what the provider does not cover", but cannot find out whether it would have worked, or try it eleven more times until the wording is natural. Review builds knowledge. It does not build the habit of using it under pressure, which is why a rep can sit through a month of call reviews and still fold on "send me an email" on Monday morning.

Review also scales badly. The manager listens to the whole call to find the ninety seconds worth discussing. At fifteen minutes per call and eight reps, one call each per week is two hours of listening for sixteen minutes of coaching.

## What a simulator does that nothing else can

A simulator gives a rep the same situation, on demand, as often as they want, with nobody watching. That is the whole case for it. A new rep who has never said "the reason for my call" out loud can say it fifty times in a week and arrive on the floor with the words automatic. A tenured rep with a weak close can run only the close, from the point where the prospect has softened, until the ask comes out clean.

The second thing a simulator does is remove the kindness. A colleague playing the prospect caves eventually. A good simulator does not. At the hardest level the prospect was interrupted mid-task, asks who is calling, challenges every claim, mentions the incumbent, and tries to end the call at least twice. It warms up only if the rep stays composed and asks a sharp question, and even then agrees to a short follow-up rather than a demo. The outcome is decided by the prospect from what was said, so a rep cannot talk themselves into a win.

The third thing is consistency of judgement. Every call is scored on the same six skills with the same definitions, and the rationale quotes the rep's own words. Two reps on the same day and one rep two weeks apart are measured against the same yardstick, which is the condition for the number meaning anything. That is what lets a manager see a dimension trending rather than remembering a call.

The levels matter more than the voice. A rep on day one needs a prospect who is friendly and has time. A rep in week three needs one who is busy and a little sceptical, who pushes on timing and budget and commits only if the discovery earns it. Only then does the cold, resistant buyer make sense. Three levels with distinct behavioural briefs is enough. A simulator with one difficulty has picked one of those reps and abandoned the others.

## What a simulator cannot do

It cannot explain. A four on objection handling with the line that earned it is useful, but the rep still needs someone to say "try asking what the provider does not cover, then stop talking". A simulator produces the evidence for that conversation. It does not have it.

It cannot know your market unless you tell it. A simulator that ships with a library of generic personas teaches generic calls. The prospect has to be built from the rep's real targets, with the pains that account has and the objections that market raises, or the fifty repetitions are repetitions of the wrong thing. The better tools go further and read a team's real call transcripts to learn how its prospects talk, which closes the gap with call review from the other side. Our [enablement page](/for-enablement) describes how grounding in your own calls works and what the grader does with it.

It cannot make a rep dial. A tool that needs a manager to schedule a session and assign a scenario is roleplay with extra steps. If reps are not opening it at ten to nine without being told, the simulator is not doing its job, however good the voice.

## Running all three without doubling the calendar

The formats are complementary because each one feeds the next. Review tells the team what the market says. The simulator turns that into a prospect and gives reps the repetitions. The scores from the simulator tell the manager which rep, which dimension and which line to talk about in roleplay. Run in that order, the manager's hours go into the parts only a manager can do.

A weekly shape we have seen hold on a floor of eight reps: Monday, fifteen minutes as a team on one real recording and the moment the call turned. Through the week, every rep runs fifteen to twenty simulator calls on their lowest-scoring skill, one level above where they are comfortable. Friday, the manager reads each rep's worst and best report, ten minutes per rep, and runs one twenty-minute roleplay with the two reps whose score did not move. Total manager time is under three hours, and the roleplays go to the reps the data says need them.

A team that only does review learns and does not practise. A team that only does roleplay practises twice a fortnight. A team that only uses a simulator gets fast at a call nobody has explained to them. The simulator carries the volume, which is why it is the one most floors are missing, and why it earns nothing on its own.

## Before trusting a simulator

Three checks in the first week of a trial. Ask the prospect "did I catch you at a bad time" and see whether it gets colder, as a buyer would, or says "no, go ahead". Have two managers score one call on paper before they see the tool's score and check it lands within a point of both. Count how many reps dialled it on the second morning without being asked. Our [buyer's guide to AI cold call practice software](/blog/ai-cold-call-practice-software) covers the features that only look good in a demo. Resistance, grounding in your targets, consistent scoring and unprompted use are the four that matter.
