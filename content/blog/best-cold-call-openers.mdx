Most cold-call training starts with the opener, and most of it is wrong. Not a little wrong: measurably, expensively wrong. When Gong analysed its dataset of over 300 million cold calls, the opener that almost every SDR is taught turned out to be the one most likely to end the call.

## The opener that kills the call

"Did I catch you at a bad time?"

It sounds polite. It gives the prospect an out. That is exactly the problem: you have just handed a stranger the easiest possible way to end the conversation, and they take it. In Gong's data, calls that opened this way were **40 percent less likely** to book a meeting than the average cold call.

"How are you today?" is only marginally better. It signals a sales call before you have said anything of substance, and it forces the prospect to perform a pleasantry with someone they did not want to speak to.

## The openers that work

Three openers stood out in the data, and they have something in common: they are honest about what the call is, and they get to the point.

**"How've you been?"**
Odd, because it implies you have spoken before. That is partly why it works: it creates a half-second of pattern interruption, the prospect's brain searches for the connection, and the tone lands as familiar instead of scripted. Gong measured a 6.6x lift in meeting rate against the baseline. Use it with a straight face and move immediately into the reason for the call.

**"I heard your name tossed around."**
A social-proof opener. It suggests the prospect has a reputation worth calling about. In the data this line had an 11.2 percent success rate, among the best measured. It only works if you can follow it with something specific to them.

**"I'll be honest, this is a cold call. Do you want to hang up, or give me thirty seconds?"**
Permission-based honesty, about 11.2 percent success as well. It pre-empts the objection the prospect was about to raise and hands them control in a way that most people, out of basic reciprocity, decline to use.

## What all three have in common

Look past the words. Each of these openers does three things in under ten seconds:

1. Names who you are without a preamble.
2. Breaks the pattern the prospect expects from a sales call.
3. Moves straight into the reason for calling.

That third point matters more than the opener itself. Gong found that stating the reason for the call explicitly, with the words "the reason I'm calling is", roughly **doubled** the success rate versus calls that drifted into a pitch. The opener buys you ten seconds. The reason for calling is what you spend them on.

## A usable template

> [Name], it's [you] from [company]. I know you weren't expecting this. The reason I'm calling is [one specific thing about their company or role]. Fair to take thirty seconds?

Specific means specific: their fleet grew, they opened a depot, they hired a new VP, they posted a job. "Companies like yours" is not a reason for calling. It is a pitch wearing a reason's clothes.

<Practise>
Run five Level 1 calls using only the template above with a different specific reason each time. Then run two Level 3 calls, where the prospect is irritated and looking for an excuse to hang up. Compare your opener score across the seven. Most reps see it move two points in an afternoon.
</Practise>
