The best cold call openers sound like a person rather than a script, and they get to the reason for the call within ten seconds. In [Gong's analysis of 90,380 cold calls](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-opening-lines), "How have you been?" booked meetings at 10.01% against a 1.5% baseline, and in its [later study of 300 million calls](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-best-and-worst-cold-call-openers-backed-by-data-from-300m-calls), "I heard your name tossed around" booked at 11.24% and a permission-based opener at 11.18%. The worst opener is "Did I catch you at a bad time?", which booked at 0.9%, 40% below baseline, because it hands the prospect the easiest exit available. The opener matters less than the line after it: stating "the reason for my call" raised success 2.1 times, a bigger effect than any opening line managed on its own.

Most cold-call training starts with the opener, and the line it teaches most often is the one that performs worst.

## The opener that kills the call

"Did I catch you at a bad time?"

It sounds considerate. It gives the prospect an out. That is exactly the problem. You have handed a stranger the easiest way to end the conversation, and they take it. In Gong's first study the line booked at 0.9%, [40% below the baseline](https://www.gong.io/cold-calling). In the later 300 million call study it was [the lowest validated opener at 2.15%](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-best-and-worst-cold-call-openers-backed-by-data-from-300m-calls). The baselines differ, so the two figures are not directly comparable, but the ranking did not move. Last both times.

"How are you today?" is only a little better. It announces a sales call before you have said anything of substance and forces the prospect to perform a pleasantry for someone they did not want to hear from. Its cousin, "How's your day going?", booked at 7.6% in the later study, well short of the best lines.

## The openers that work, with their rates

| Opener | Booking rate | Study |
|---|---|---|
| "How have you been?" | 10.01%, against a 1.5% baseline | 90,380 calls |
| "I heard your name tossed around" | 11.24% | 300M+ calls, 2024 |
| "This is a cold call. Do you want to hang up, or give me thirty seconds?" | 11.18% | 300M+ calls, 2024 |
| "How's your day going?" | 7.6% | 300M+ calls, 2024 |
| "Did I catch you at a bad time?" | 0.9% in the first study, 2.15% in the second | Both |

All figures are from Gong Labs. The two studies use different baselines, so compare rank rather than rate across them. The full set with sources is on our [cold calling statistics](/blog/cold-calling-statistics) page.

Three openers stood out, and all three get to the point.

**"How've you been?"** Odd, because it implies you have spoken before. That is partly why it works. It creates a half-second of pattern interruption, and the tone lands as familiar instead of scripted. Against a 1.5% baseline, 10.01% is a 6.6x lift. Use it with a straight face and move immediately into the reason for the call.

**"I heard your name tossed around."** A social-proof opener. It suggests the prospect has a reputation worth a call. It only works if you can follow it with something specific to them, a project they ran, a post they wrote, a team they built. Followed by a generic pitch it tells the prospect you were bluffing.

**"This is a cold call. Do you want to hang up, or give me thirty seconds?"** Permission-based honesty. It pre-empts the objection the prospect was about to raise and hands them control in a way most people, out of basic reciprocity, decline to use. Thirty seconds is the right ask.

## What the opener buys and what spends it

Each of the three does the same three things in under ten seconds. It names who you are without a preamble. It breaks the pattern the prospect expects from a sales call. And it moves straight into the reason for calling.

The third matters more than the opener itself. Gong found that stating the reason for the call explicitly, with the words "the reason I'm calling is", [raised success 2.1 times](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-call-opening-lines). That is a bigger effect than the gap between a good opener and an average one. The opener buys you ten seconds of attention. The reason for the call is what you spend them on, and we have a separate post on [how to build the reason for the call](/blog/reason-for-call).

A usable template puts both in one breath:

> [Name], it's [you] from [company]. I know you weren't expecting this. The reason I'm calling is [one specific thing about their company or role]. Fair to take thirty seconds?

Swap the first sentence for "How've you been?" if that suits your voice. Do not swap the second half for anything.

## A worked exchange

Here is the shape on a resistant prospect, interrupted mid-task and looking for a reason to hang up.

> Rep: Sarah, it's Tom at Fleetwise. I'll be honest, this is a cold call. Do you want to hang up, or can I take thirty seconds?
>
> Prospect: Who is this? How did you get my number?
>
> Rep: Tom, Fleetwise. From your company page. The reason I'm calling is you opened a second depot in Leeds last month, and the two teams we work with who did that both had the same problem with driver hours in the first quarter. Fair to take thirty seconds?
>
> Prospect: Go on. You've got thirty seconds.

The rep did not answer "how did you get my number" with an apology or a paragraph. One phrase, then back to the reason for the call. The prospect pushed twice and the rep stayed on the same line both times.

Now the same prospect with the opener most reps are taught.

> Rep: Hi Sarah, it's Tom from Fleetwise. Did I catch you at a bad time?
>
> Prospect: Yes, actually. Can you send me something?
>
> Rep: Of course, what's the best email?

Call over, and the rep logged it as a follow-up.

## The mistake that undoes a good opener

The commonest way to waste a good opener, in our calls, is the company paragraph. The rep lands "How've you been?", gets the half-second of curiosity, and spends it on "we're a leading provider of fleet management solutions for mid-market logistics firms". The curiosity is gone by "provider".

Specific means specific. Their fleet grew, they opened a depot, they hired a new VP, they posted a job. "Companies like yours" is not a reason for calling. It is a pitch wearing a reason's clothes. If you cannot name one thing about this prospect that justifies the call, the opener will not save it.

## The benchmark for a team

Two Gong figures set the bar. Average reps turn [4.6% of connects into a meeting and top reps 16.7%](https://www.gong.io/files/gong-guide-how-to-master-cold-calls.pdf), and the [median successful cold call lasts 4.8 minutes and handles at least two objections](https://www.gong.io/blog/we-found-the-top-objections-across-300m-cold-calls-heres-how-to-handle-them-all). The opener is the first fifteen seconds of that.

For a manager the useful view is the team's opener, not one rep's. If six reps out of eight open with "bad time", that is a script problem, fixed by changing one line in the playbook. If one rep does, it is a coaching conversation. The [page for sales managers](/for-managers) shows opener scores next to the other five skills across a team, weakest first.
