A cold call review that changes what a rep does next takes fifteen minutes and works from the transcript, not the recording. You pick the call on purpose, read to the line where the call turned, quote that line back to the rep, ask what the prospect heard, agree one replacement and have the rep say it aloud. Then you check the same skill on their next five calls. Most call reviews fail because they do the opposite. The manager listens to a stack of recordings, comments on everything, and the rep leaves with a list of eight notes and no idea which one mattered. One line, one change, checked, is the whole method.

## Why most call reviews change nothing

The usual review is a Friday block. The manager pulls up the week's recordings, listens to five or six in a row, and writes notes as things occur to them. Then they sit down with the rep and go through the notes. Three things go wrong before anyone speaks.

The manager reviews from impression. Listening to audio at speed produces a feeling about the call, and the notes record the feeling. "Sounded hesitant at the start." "Lost them around the middle." Neither points to a line the rep can change.

The manager reviews everything. Eight notes across six skills feel thorough. The rep hears the first two, braces for the rest, and remembers the tone of the conversation rather than its content.

The manager reviews the outcome. A booked meeting gets praise, a hang-up gets scrutiny, and the review drifts toward whether the call worked instead of what the rep did. A rep who booked a meeting with a weak close has been told the close was fine. A rep who ran a good call against a prospect who was never going to agree has been told to try harder.

The fix for all three is the same. Review one thing, from the transcript, by the line.

## Which call to pick

Do not review the best call and do not pick at random. Pick the call where the skill you are working on this week got tested. If the rep's weak dimension is objection handling, you want a call where the prospect pushed back at least twice. If it is the close, you want a call that got far enough for an ask. A call that ended at the opener tells you nothing about the rep's discovery.

Three picks work well in rotation. One the rep chooses, because the call a rep wants to talk about is usually the one that bothered them. One where the objection your team fumbles most came up, because that is where the floor loses meetings. And one where the prospect ended the call early, because the line before a hang-up is the most honest feedback a rep ever gets.

On cadence, we review one call a day for a ramping rep in their first month and one or two a week for tenured reps. That is our observation from running SDR floors, not a benchmark. The important thing is that the number is small enough to do properly. Choosing from a day's calls is a five-minute job with transcripts and an afternoon with recordings.

## The fifteen minutes, by the clock

| Minute | What happens | Who talks |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 2 | The rep says which skill they were working on and where they think the call turned | Rep |
| 2 to 6 | Both read the transcript in silence to the turning point. No audio. | Nobody |
| 6 to 10 | The manager quotes the rep's line and asks "what did the prospect hear?" and "what did they say next?" | Rep, mostly |
| 10 to 13 | Agree one replacement line. The rep says it out loud, twice, as they would on a call | Rep |
| 13 to 15 | Write the change in one sentence on the call. Set the check: same skill, next five calls | Manager |

The silence in minutes two to six is the part managers skip and should not. Reading together puts the manager and the rep on the same evidence before either has an opinion. It also stops the manager narrating the call as it plays, which is where most of the eight notes come from.

Saying the new line aloud, twice, is the other part that gets skipped. A rep who nods at a suggestion has agreed with it. A rep who has said it twice can produce it under pressure tomorrow.

## Finding the line where the call turned

Every cold call that goes wrong has a line where it went wrong, and it is almost always the rep's line, not the prospect's. The prospect's objection is the response. The cause is the sentence before it. These are the patterns to look for, by skill.

| Skill | The turning line usually looks like | What the prospect says next | The question to ask the rep |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opener | "Did I catch you at a bad time?" or "How are you today?" | "Who is this?" or "What's this about?" | What did they know about you after your first sentence? |
| Reason for call | "We help companies like yours with…" | "Not interested" or "Send me an email" | Could you have read that line to anyone on the list? |
| Discovery | A question, the prospect answers, and the rep pitches instead of following up | A shorter answer, then silence | What did they just tell you, and what did you do with it? |
| Objection handling | The prospect says "we already have something" and the rep lists features | "Like I said, we're covered" | What was the actual concern behind that line? |
| Value proposition | Three capabilities in one breath, none tied to anything the prospect said | "OK" with no question back | Which problem of theirs was that sentence about? |
| Close | "Can I send you some information?" or "Would sometime next week work?" | "Sure, send it over" or "Maybe" | What exactly did you ask them to do, and when? |

Two numbers help find the line faster when you have them. Time to first objection tells you whether the opener and the reason for the call held. A prospect who objects in the first ten seconds is responding to the opener. One who objects after forty is responding to the pitch. Longest monologue tells you where discovery stopped. Our post on [how much an SDR should talk on a cold call](/blog/cold-call-talk-ratio) explains why the longest stretch matters more than the overall ratio.

## What to say, and what not to

Quote the line. Not "you were a bit generic early on" but "you said 'we help companies like yours improve outbound efficiency'. What did she hear?" The rep can argue with a characterisation. They cannot argue with a transcript.

Ask before telling. "What did the prospect hear" and "what did they say next" do most of the work, because the rep usually sees the problem once they read the exchange cold. A change the rep found is one they will make. A change the manager announced is one they will remember being told.

One change, stated as a line, not as advice. "Be more specific" is advice. "The reason for my call is that you took on twelve SDRs in September and most teams that do that hit a ramp problem by November" is a line. The replacement should be short enough to say in one breath and specific enough that the rep could not have used it on a different prospect.

Skip the sandwich. Opening with praise the rep knows is a preamble teaches them to wait for the "but". If the call had a good moment, say so afterwards, in one sentence, and name the line.

Do not review the score. If you use a scorecard, the number is how you chose the call and how you will check the change. It is not the subject of the conversation. A rep who is told "you got a four on objection handling" has learned a number. A rep who is shown the line and the prospect's reply has learned what a four is.

## Checking it worked

The review is not finished when the meeting ends. It is finished when you have looked at the same skill on the next five calls. Five is enough to see whether the line is being used and whether the prospect's response changed. One call proves nothing either way.

Track the skill, not the call. If the dimension score moved up across five calls, keep the change and pick the next skill. If it did not, the change was the wrong one, which is worth knowing, and the next review starts from the same line with a different replacement.
