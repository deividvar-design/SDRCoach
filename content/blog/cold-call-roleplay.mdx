Cold call roleplay that reps do not hate has four properties: no audience, one skill per session, a prospect who can say no, and the same short rubric on every call. The usual format fails on all four. One rep performs a whole call in front of the team, against a manager who knows the pitch and caves at the end, and gets feedback that changes with the manager's mood. Reps do not hate practice. They hate being watched, judged inconsistently and given one turn a fortnight, and each of those is a choice about format rather than a fact about roleplay.

Managers mostly know this. They know roleplay works, they know nobody does it, and they have stopped scheduling it. Reps agree there is not enough of it: in Salesforce's State of Sales survey, 41% of sales professionals said they do not get enough roleplay and 46% said they rarely get feedback on their conversations ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/blog/sales/effectively-coach-sales-team-blog/)). The fix is not more of the usual version. It is a different exercise.

## Why the usual version fails

**There is an audience.** The standard format is one rep, one manager playing the prospect, and the rest of the team watching. The rep is not practising a cold call. They are performing one, in front of the people whose opinion matters most to them. Nobody takes a risk in that setting. They run the safest version of their script and hope it ends quickly.

**The manager plays the prospect.** Managers make bad prospects. They know the product too well, so they raise the objections the pitch is designed to handle. They know the rep, so they either go easy or decide today is the day to make a point. And because they are playing the prospect, they cannot also watch the rep. They come out with an impression rather than notes.

**There is no repetition.** A rep gets one turn, maybe two, in a session that happens every other week if the calendar holds. Nobody has ever got good at anything with two attempts a fortnight. Compare it with the real thing: phone-centric SDR teams make 56 dials a day ([The Bridge Group](https://www.bridgegroupinc.com/research/2025-sdr-models-metrics-report-the-bridge-group), 2025). Roleplay at that cadence is not practice, it is assessment.

**Nothing is at stake, and everything is.** The prospect always caves eventually, because the manager wants to get through the exercise, so the rep learns that persistence gets rewarded. Meanwhile the social stakes are high, so the rep learns to dread it. The incentives are backwards.

## What to change

**Take the audience away.** Practice should be private by default. A rep should be able to run the same objection twelve times in a row, badly, without anyone watching. Share the good one afterwards if it helps the team. The failures stay between the rep and whoever is coaching them.

**One skill per session.** Do not run "a cold call". Run the first thirty seconds, twenty times. Run "we already have a solution", ten times. Run the close, from the point where the prospect has softened. A full call is too much to change at once, and the feedback ends up as a list of eight things, of which the rep will remember none. Pick the dimension with the lowest score and drill that until it moves.

**Use levels.** A new rep should start against a prospect who is friendly and has time, because the point is to get the words out in order. Once the words are automatic, the prospect gets busier and more sceptical. Only then do you introduce the senior buyer who was interrupted mid-task and wants you gone. Jumping straight to the hard prospect teaches new reps to freeze. Staying on the easy one teaches tenured reps nothing.

**The prospect decides.** The most important change. In real life the rep does not get to decide whether the call worked. The prospect does, based on what was actually said. Roleplay should work the same way. The prospect agrees to a next step only if it was earned, and the session ends with a clear outcome: meeting, callback, or no. A roleplay where the prospect always comes round is a rehearsal for a world that does not exist.

## A weekly format that holds

| Session | Who | How long | What is scored |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily drill, Monday to Thursday | Rep alone, against an AI prospect or a peer off camera | 15 minutes, five calls | One dimension only, the rep's lowest from last week |
| Level step-up, midweek | Rep alone, one level harder than they are comfortable with | 15 minutes, five calls | The same dimension. Did the score hold under more resistance |
| Transcript read, Thursday | Rep alone | 10 minutes | Nothing. The rep writes down the one line they would change |
| One-to-one, Friday | Rep and manager | 20 minutes | The line the rep brought, plus the dimension's trend over the week |
| Team share, fortnightly | Whole team | 15 minutes | Nothing. One good call played, one thing named, no critique |

The drills are where the learning happens and the Friday one-to-one is where it gets explained. The timings are our observation, not a benchmark. The rep gets twenty-five attempts a week at one skill instead of one attempt at everything, and the manager's twenty minutes go on a line the rep chose rather than on playing a prospect. Managers already spend about 36 minutes a week coaching each rep, and 77% of them think their reps get too little ([Sales Management Association](https://salesmanagement.org/web/uploads/pdf-renamed-by-uzzal/89f5f8ff556f7a60adf7f1a78eac94c1.pdf), 2015). The format fits inside that.

Daily matters more than long. Retention of newly learned material falls to about 32% after a day and 4% after a month without reinforcement ([Murre and Dros](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0120644), PLOS ONE, 2015). A fortnightly session teaches almost nothing that survives to the next one.

## Scoring it consistently

Roleplay feedback is usually "that was good" plus whatever the manager happened to notice. Two managers watching the same call give different notes. The same manager gives different notes on Monday and Friday. That inconsistency is why reps do not trust it.

Score every call against the same rubric, and make it short. Six dimensions is about right: opener, reason for the call, discovery, objection handling, value, close. For each, a number and one sentence that quotes what the rep said. "Objection handling, 4: when the prospect said 'we've got something', you went straight to features instead of asking what they had." Our [cold call scorecard template](/blog/cold-call-scorecard-template) has the six lines and the anchors for each score.

Two rules make the scores mean something. Score from the transcript, not from memory. If you cannot point at the line, it does not go in the notes. And be stingy. If a nine is common, the score tells the rep nothing. The scale should leave room above where your best rep sits today.

Then track the dimension over time, not the call. Five calls with the same weak dimension is a coaching priority. One bad call is a Tuesday. Coaching what the rep does on the call is also where the return is: coaching reps on activities lifted productivity 24%, against 10% for coaching on results ([Sales Management Association](https://salesmanagement.org/blog/sales-coaching-your-greatest-lever-on-quota/), 515 managers, 2018).

## Where AI practice changes the maths

Everything above can be done with a manager, a rep and a rubric. What it cannot be is done at volume. A manager with eight reps who plays the prospect for twenty minutes per rep per week spends nearly three hours to give each rep one attempt.

An AI prospect changes the arithmetic without changing the method. The rep can run the same objection fifteen times before lunch. The prospect is built from real target accounts, so it raises the objections those buyers raise, not the ones in the pitch deck. It does not know the rep, it does not go easy, and it decides the outcome from what was said. Every call comes back as a transcript with the prospect's decision, and the rep can ask for a scored coach's review. We set the formats side by side in [cold call simulator versus roleplay](/blog/cold-call-simulator).

What it does not do is replace the manager. The rep still needs someone to read the transcript with them and say "here, this line, try it this way". What changes is that the manager arrives already knowing which dimension is weak, which objection keeps going wrong, and the exact line where it happens. The twenty minutes go on coaching rather than on listening for something to coach.
