Ask who owns the problem and what they would need to see. "I'm not the decision maker" is often true, and the answer is not to pitch harder at the wrong person. Ask who feels the problem most and what that person is measured on, then whether you can mention this conversation when you call them. A name, a reason to call and a warm handover is a win.

## What they actually mean

Three readings. The literal one: they do not sign for this and are saying so to save you both time. The deflection: they could influence it but do not want the conversation, and the boss is a convenient exit. The loyal one: they are protecting a colleague from a cold call, which is decent of them.

The honest version offers a name unprompted, with some detail about why. The brush-off is vague, "someone in ops, probably", and goes no further. One question about the problem, not the org chart, tells you which. A daily user has a view on what is wrong with the tool. If they share it, they are a source. If they shut down, they were never going to help.

Either way, you are in discovery with someone who has no reason to posture, because nobody is selling to them. That is rare and worth two minutes. It sounds like [why are you calling me](/objections/why-are-you-calling-me), but that one asks you to justify the call and this one to redirect it. Of [the objections reps hear most](/blog/common-cold-call-objections), in our observation it is the one they give up on soonest.

## Three ways to handle it

### Ask who owns the problem, not the signature

The default. "Decision maker" is about process. "Owns the problem" is about who is losing sleep.

> That's fine, I'd rather talk to the right person. Who feels this most when it goes wrong?

### Ask what they would need to see

Use this once you have a name. It turns a referral into a brief.

> When I call them, what would make it worth their time? What are they measured on?

### Get the problem from the user before you go

Use this when the person on the line uses the thing every day. They know where it breaks.

> Before I go, you're the one living with it. What's the bit that annoys you most?

## An exchange that works

> **Prospect:** I'm not the decision maker on this.
>
> **Rep:** That's fine, I'd rather speak to the right person. Who feels it most when ramp goes slowly?
>
> **Prospect:** Our head of sales. She runs the SDR team day to day.
>
> **Rep:** What's she measured on? Pipeline, or time to first deal?
>
> **Prospect:** Time to first deal. She gets asked about it every month.
>
> **Rep:** Useful. Can I say we spoke when I call her, and that it's about time to first deal?
>
> **Prospect:** Sure. Tuesday mornings are best.
>
> **Rep:** Tuesday morning it is. Thanks, that's a real help.

A coach would score this well on discovery and on closing. The rep did not push through the wrong person. One question found who owns the problem, one what she is measured on, and one asked permission to use the conversation. The next step is concrete: a role, a day and a topic.

## The mistakes that lose the call

| What reps say | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| "Could you put me through to them?" | It makes the prospect the switchboard. Most say no to protect a colleague, and the call ends with nothing. |
| "Who is the decision maker?" | An org-chart question. It gets a title, not a problem. The person who feels it often does not sign. |
| Pitching anyway "so you can pass it on" | They will not pass it on, and if they do it arrives garbled. Give them one sentence, not a pitch. |
| Hanging up at the first "not me" | You had someone who uses the product and no reason to posture. That was the best discovery of the day. |

## Say this, not that

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "Who's the decision maker?" | "Who feels this most when it goes wrong?" |
| "Can you transfer me?" | "Can I mention we spoke when I call them?" |
| "Could you pass this on?" | "What would make it worth their time?" |
| "OK, thanks anyway" | "Before I go, what's the bit that annoys you most?" |

<Practise>
Start with the Level 1 warm-up prospect, which answers readily. Aim to leave with a name, what they are measured on, and permission to mention the call, then run the same questions against Level 3.
</Practise>
