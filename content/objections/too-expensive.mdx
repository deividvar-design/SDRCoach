"Too expensive" on a cold call means the prospect heard a number before they heard a reason to care about it. The answer is not to defend the number and not to drop it. Go back to the cost of the problem: "Fair. Before we talk about price, what is the problem costing you now, roughly?" Then give one result a similar team got. A price is only too high against nothing. Against a problem with a figure on it, and a peer who fixed it for about the same money, the same number reads differently.

## What they actually mean

Price is always a comparison. Early in a cold call the prospect has nothing to compare your number with except doing nothing, and against doing nothing every number loses. Sometimes the objection is sticker shock from a figure quoted too soon, which is usually the rep's doing. Sometimes it is a genuine comparison with a competitor or with the tool they have. And sometimes it is a soft no dressed up as a budget fact, because "too expensive" is harder to argue with than "not interested".

The honest version comes with curiosity. They ask what is included, or they name the figure they would pay, or they tell you what the other option costs. The brush-off is flat: "too expensive", no number, no follow-up question. If you offer to go back to the problem and they will not, you have your answer.

It is worth separating this from [no budget](/objections/no-budget), which sits next to it on our list of [common cold-call objections](/blog/common-cold-call-objections). No budget is a timing problem. They may want it and cannot pay this quarter. Too expensive is a value problem. They have money, they do not yet believe your number buys enough.

## Three ways to handle it

### Go back to the cost of the problem

The default, and the one to use whenever the price came up before any discovery.

> Fair. Park the price for a second. What is the problem costing you at the moment, roughly, in hours or in money?

### Give one peer result

Use this once you have a figure for the problem, or when the prospect is senior and wants evidence rather than questions. Use a real result from a real customer, with the detail that makes it believable. One is enough.

> A team about your size spent roughly the same and cut ramp time from eight weeks to five.
>
> Would that be worth it to you?

### Ask what they are comparing it to

Use this when they say a competitor is cheaper, or when they give you a number of their own. It brings the real comparison into the open, including the cost of the thing they already have.

> Compared to what, out of interest?
>
> If it is the tool you have now, what does that cost you once you count the time?

## An exchange that works

> **Prospect:** We looked at something like this last year. Too expensive.
>
> **Rep:** Fair. What was the problem you were trying to fix at the time?
>
> **Prospect:** New reps taking too long to get to quota. Still the case, if I am honest.
>
> **Rep:** How long is too long?
>
> **Prospect:** Four months, give or take. Should be two.
>
> **Rep:** So two months of salary per hire where they are not producing. A team near you with the same gap got it under three. Worth fifteen minutes to see how, Wednesday at nine?
>
> **Prospect:** Go on then.

A coach would score this well on discovery and on objection handling. The rep did not touch the price for the whole exchange. Two short questions got the prospect to size the problem in their own words, and the rep offered one peer result instead of a pitch. The next step was specific and small, which is the right size for a prospect who is still weighing cost.

## The mistakes that lose the call

| What reps say | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "We can do a discount if that helps." | Confirms the price was padded, and you still have not established why they would pay anything. |
| "It is actually very competitive." | Defending the number. You are now arguing about the one thing you cannot change on the call. |
| Quoting in the first minute | You created the objection. Nothing on the call has earned a number yet. |
| "What is your budget?" | On a cold call they will not tell you, and asking marks you as someone working out how much to charge. |
| "You get what you pay for." | Insulting. It implies their current choice is cheap and bad, and they chose it. |

## Say this, not that

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "I can probably get you a better price." | "Before price, what is the problem costing you now?" |
| "It is cheaper than you think." | "A team your size got that result for about this. Would that be worth it?" |
| "What is your budget?" | "What are you comparing it to?" |
| "It pays for itself." | "If it cut that four months to three, what would that be worth per hire?" |

The Level 2 inbound prospect asks about price early and pushes back on whatever you say. Practise not quoting until you have a figure for the problem, then watch what happens to the objection when you finally do.
